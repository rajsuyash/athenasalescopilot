# Tonality and Delivery

The reframe library tells you *what to say*. This file tells you *how to
say it*. Tonality is what makes the difference between a reframe that
lands and one that reads as a script.

---

## The four tonalities

Per the source operator (Reel 30), every sales call rotates through four
tonalities. Mastering when to switch is the whole skill.

| Tonality | When to use | Sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| **Casual** | Disarms, transitions, when you want to lower their guard | *"Yeah no, not a problem at all."* |
| **Curious** | Probing, uncovering, asking why | *"Wait — why do you say that?"* |
| **Skeptical** | Challenging weak claims they made | *"10% conversion? Why so low?"* |
| **Concerned** | Bringing weight to the consequence step | *"What does that mean for the next 6 months for you?"* |

The mistake most reps make: they stay in one tonality the whole call.
The reframe loop needs all four — disarm in casual, uncover in curious,
reframe in casual, justify in curious, consequence in concerned, close
in casual.

---

## Hot content, cold tone

This is the single most important delivery rule.

The hotter the content of a line, the colder you deliver it. Examples
from the source:

- *"I don't have a gun to your head"* — said warmly, almost amused.
  Delivered intensely, this would land as aggressive. Casual delivery
  flips it into a permission-giving disarm.
- *"It's $3 million"* (Reel 23) — said with a half-laugh after a
  question about price. The absurd content + flat tone makes the
  prospect chuckle instead of recoil.
- *"You're trying to fucking rob me"* (Reel 34, prospect's line, but
  the rep's casual response is what defuses it).

**Mechanism:** Aggressive content delivered aggressively triggers the
prospect's threat response. Aggressive content delivered casually
confuses the threat response — the brain can't reconcile *"this should
sting"* with *"but his tone says it's fine."* Result: the prospect
takes it as a peer comment, not an attack.

**Application for B2B / professional register:** Drop the aggressive
content entirely; keep the casual delivery on hard questions.
*"What's actually more expensive — the platform, or another 6 months
of the current setup?"* — said as if you're musing aloud, not pitching.

## The pause

The most underused tool. From Reel 46:

> "Your pacing is everything. If you just go *yeah, what's more
> expensive man, is it more expensive if you do this or that*, you're
> not giving them time to process. You need to slow down and pause
> whenever you're using these important phrases because it gives their
> brain time to actually process what you're saying."

**Where to pause:**

- **Before a binary.** *"Two kinds of people. [pause] The first one…"*
- **Inside a binary.** *"…there's the first guy. [pause] He's always
  looking for perfection. [pause] And there's the second guy…"*
- **After the close question.** Don't fill the silence. The first one
  to speak loses leverage.

**How long:** 1.5–3 seconds. Long enough that the prospect notices the
weight of what's coming next. Short enough that it doesn't feel
theatrical.

## "Between you and I"

A status-equalizer. Used before any hard or intimate question.

Source examples:
- *"Between you and I, on a scale of 1 to 10, where do you feel you
  actually are?"* (Reel 2)
- *"Between you and I, just to be completely real, would you really
  want me to train your entire sales team if I just said *have a great
  day*?"* (Reel 9)
- *"Between you and I, do you really feel this is the answer for what
  you're looking for?"* (Reel 25)

**Why it works:** Frames the upcoming question as off-the-record peer
talk, not sales talk. Lowers the prospect's defensive posture by ~30%.
Then you ask the real question.

**Variants:** *"Just so I understand…"* / *"Real talk…"* / *"Off
the record…"* — same function.

## "Not a problem at all"

The universal disarm. Used after every objection before any reframe.

The trick is *intonation*. It needs to sound like the objection didn't
register as an objection — like you're agreeing with a request to
reschedule, not parrying a sales pushback.

**Wrong:** *"Not a problem at all"* (delivered tight, defensive,
forced-friendly).

**Right:** *"Yeah, not a problem at all"* (delivered loose, slightly
distracted, like you're already thinking about the next thing).

The looseness signals: *I am not invested in your decision. I'm not
desperate for this. The pressure you feel is your own.* That signal
is what lets the reframe land.

---

## "Can I make a suggestion?" / "Can I push back on that?"

Permission-asking before challenging. From Reels 9, 13, 32, 34.

**Why it works:** They almost always say yes. Once they've said yes,
they've granted you permission to push, which means they can't get
defensive when you do. You've moved them from passive recipient to
active participant.

**Use before:**
- Any reframe more aggressive than the average tone of the call.
- Calling out a contradiction in their stated position.
- Offering an alternative interpretation of their objection.

**Don't overuse.** Twice per call max. More than that and it becomes
a tic.

## Self-deprecation as trust

From Reel 43:

> "Whenever somebody lies, people assume it's for their own benefit.
> So if you tell somebody a lie with a detail that is vulnerable about
> you or makes you look bad, they assume it must be true. Getting
> people to trust you works the exact same way."

**Application:** A small, specific, vulnerable disclosure early in the
objection-handling phase signals you're not in pure sales mode. *"I'm
not going to pretend I haven't lost deals to that exact concern,"* or
*"Honestly, this same objection was the one that almost killed our
last enterprise rollout."*

**Caveat:** Specific vulnerability builds trust. Generic "humble brag"
vulnerability erodes it. *"My biggest weakness is I work too hard"*
is not this technique.

## The "Why?"

The most-skipped, most-important word in the loop.

After every reframe, the prospect will agree with the new frame *if*
the reframe was good. The temptation is to move on to the close. Don't.

Ask: *"Why?"*

Make them defend the new belief in their own words.

Source examples:
- *"Yeah, but why?"* — used 8+ times in the corpus.
- *"Why do you think that is?"* (Reel 26)
- *"Why so low?"* (Reel 28, used to tear at an ego claim)

The justification step is the lock. Without it, the new belief is fragile
and the prospect will revert to the original objection within hours.
With it, the belief is theirs.

## "What's behind that?"

The peeling tool. Surface objections almost always cover deeper ones.
*"It's too expensive"* often covers *"I don't believe I can pull this
off."* The tool to peel: *"What's behind that?"*

From Reel 16 (theatrical example, but the move is real):

> Surface: *"No."*
> Rep: *"What's behind that no?"*
> Layer 2: *"It's about what people would think."*
> Rep: *"So it's not about the kiss, it's about social perception."*
> Layer 3: *"It's just weird."*
> Rep: *"So it's not that it's wrong, it's outside your comfort zone."*

**Pattern:** Each *"what's behind that"* peels one layer. Stop peeling
when the prospect names something specific and concrete — that's the
real objection. Then handle that one.

## Reduce-to-ridiculous

Used when the prospect's stated objection is technically true but
practically absurd. Make the absurdity visible without insulting them.

Source examples:
- *"Is your wife going to be on the calls with you, like *honey, say
  this say that*? Or is that going to be you?"* (Reel 36) — for
  partner objections.
- *"If you had a fat person who said *I'll start working out after I
  lose the weight*, what would you tell that person?"* (Reel 6) — for
  acting-as-if reframes.
- *"If your dog or your tree was making this decision, would the
  business be better off?"* (Reel 32) — for outsourced-decision
  objections.

**Rule:** The reductio has to be funny enough that they laugh, not so
absurd that they feel mocked. The line is fine. When in doubt, stay
on their side of it.

## Silence after the close

From Reel 44:

> "Step 1: Ask question. Step 2: Stop talking. Step 3: Wait 3 seconds
> before you speak."

After you ask the identity-close question — *"What decision do you
feel like you need to make to put yourself in the best possible
position?"* — stop talking. Do not fill the silence. Do not soften
the question. Do not offer an out.

Most reps lose deals here by adding qualifiers: *"I mean, you don't
have to decide right now…"* That qualifier hands them an exit.

The prospect will sit with the question. If you've run the loop right,
they'll answer with a version of *"I just have to do it."* That answer
is the close.

## Voice-agent specific tonality

Voice agents lose two things: vocal warmth and the casual tonality
that disarms. To compensate:

- **Shorter sentences.** Long sentences in a synthetic voice sound
  robotic; short ones sound deliberate.
- **More pauses, but flagged.** Real humans pause naturally. Synthetic
  voices need to be told to pause. Insert `<break time="1.5s"/>` or
  equivalent at every pause point.
- **No casual language.** *"Yeah no, not a problem at all"* sounds
  off in synthetic voice. Use *"Of course. Let me ask you this —"*
  instead.
- **No analogies.** The "Heavy is the Crown" story doesn't work in
  a voice-agent context — the prospect will hang up. Use the binary
  reduce-to-ridiculous version.
- **Explicit confirmation loops.** After every reframe, the agent
  should confirm the prospect's response before moving on. Humans
  can read tone; agents need verbal confirmation.

## Anti-patterns to avoid

- **Closer-bro energy.** "Bro," "fam," "let's get it" — alienating in
  any professional register. The framework doesn't need it.
- **Stacking reframes.** Two binaries back-to-back ("two kinds of
  people… and also, there are two ways to look at money…") reads as a
  script. Land one, justify it, close.
- **Rushing the loop.** A real reframe loop takes 2–4 minutes. Trying
  to do it in 30 seconds compresses out the pauses, the justify step,
  and the consequence step — and the loop collapses.
- **Reframing without isolating first.** If you reframe a smokescreen
  objection, you've solved the wrong problem. Always isolate the real
  concern before reframing.
- **Adding a feature pitch into the reframe.** *"Two kinds of people…
  and by the way, our platform also has a 99.9% uptime SLA."* Don't.
  The reframe is its own thing. Features come later, if at all.
